### **Introduction**

Africa faces a rapidly escalating waste challenge driven by urbanization, population growth, economic development, and changing consumption patterns. Waste types now span solid, liquid, and gaseous streams, each posing complex environmental, public health, and socioeconomic risks.

Without accurate, harmonized data, governments, investors, researchers, and communities are unable to plan, finance, and implement effective waste and pollution management systems.

The Africa Waste Observatory (AWO) serves as a central hub for consolidated continental waste data, performance indicators, and transformative strategies to drive a cleaner, healthier, and circular Africa.

### **The Waste Challenge in Africa**

* **Low waste collection and treatment coverage**, especially in informal, peri-urban, and rural areas.  
* **Widespread open dumping**, open burning, and untreated liquid discharge.  
* **Weak enforcement** of waste, water, and emissions regulations.  
* **Limited recycling**, wastewater treatment, and gaseous emissions controls.  
* **Severe lack of accurate, comparable waste and emissions data** continent-wide.

### **Our Solution**

The Africa Waste Observatory provides:

* Standardized continental data on solid, liquid, gaseous and other kinds of waste.  
* Interactive dashboards for policy formulation, investment planning, and monitoring.  
* Country profiles detailing generation, treatment, recycling, and emissions.  
* Policy-relevant analysis and practical, scalable solutions.

### **What We Deliver**

* Country-level waste profiles covering all waste streams.  
* Waste generation and treatment data.  
* Collection coverage and performance indicators.  
* Policy briefs, benchmarking, and best-practice sharing.  
* Recommendations tailored to governments, cities, and stakeholders.

### **Impact Goals**

* Cleaner cities and communities.  
* Improved public health outcomes.  
* Increased recycling, treatment, and resource recovery.  
* Reduced environmental pollution.  
* Accelerated transition to a circular economy.

## **2\. ABOUT PAGE**

### **About Africa Waste Observatory (AWO)**

The Africa Waste Observatory is dedicated to improving waste management across all waste streams — solid, liquid, and gaseous — through data, knowledge sharing, and capacity development.

AWO is aligned with global and regional initiatives supported by UNEP, African Development Bank partners, UNECA, and national environmental agencies.

### **Why We Exist**

Effective waste management depends on accurate data and informed decision-making. Across Africa, data is often fragmented, outdated, or inconsistent, making it difficult to allocate resources, design effective policies, or measure progress. AWO was created to fill this critical gap.

### **Our Mission**

To support African countries in building efficient, inclusive, and sustainable waste and pollution management systems using harmonized data, analysis, and practical tools.

### **Our Vision**

A clean, resilient, and circular Africa where waste and emissions are minimized, resources are recovered, and cities are environmentally sustainable.

### **Core Objectives**

* Improve waste management systems (solid, liquid, gaseous).  
* Promote integrated waste management based on the hierarchy: reduce, reuse, recycle, treat, dispose.  
* Support evidence-based policies and investment plans.  
* Guide financial institutions on sustainable infrastructure.  
* Strengthen regional capacity and collaboration.

### **How AWO Works**

* **Data Collection:** From national environment agencies, utilities, municipalities, researchers, and partners.  
* **Harmonization:** Standardized indicators and definitions.  
* **Analysis & Reporting:** Translate raw data into actionable insights.  
* **Knowledge Sharing:** Disseminate best practices, case studies, and tools.

## **3\. COUNTRY PROFILES**

### **SOUTH AFRICA**

* **Overview:** A middle-income waste profile generating approximately 122 million tonnes annually. It leads the regional market (40% share) with a strong legislative focus on a "Just Transition" that includes informal waste pickers in the 2025 National Determined Contributions (NDC).  
* **Collection Systems:** High coverage in urban centers (\~80-90%) but significant gaps in informal settlements. The system is increasingly integrated with "District Development Models" to optimize municipal service delivery.  
* **Recycling and Innovation:** Features the continent’s most advanced Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) frameworks. Innovation is centered on e-waste (new 2025 National Policy) and polymer recovery, which saw over 430,000 tonnes recycled recently.  
* **Disposal Practices:** Heavily reliant on landfills, many of which are at critical capacity. Efforts are shifting toward methane reduction and capturing landfill gas to meet 2025 climate targets.  
* **Priority Actions:**  
  * Phase out single-use plastics through the 2025 NDC policy directives.  
  * Formalize waste pickers through "pay-per-kg" incentives and health/safety integration.  
  * Implement decentralized organic waste systems to reduce methane emissions.

### **EGYPT**

* **Overview:** Generates roughly 100 million tonnes of total waste annually. The National Solid Waste Management Programme (NSWMP) is currently driving a massive institutional overhaul to move away from legacy dumping.  
* **Collection Systems:** Urban collection is robust (\~80-85% in major cities) but rural coverage lags at \~35%. The Zaballeen (informal collectors) remain the backbone of the Cairo metropolitan collection system.  
* **Recycling and Innovation:** Large-scale manual sorting leads to high diversion rates in specific hubs. Innovation focuses on Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) and the establishment of an Integrated Solid Waste Management Sector (ISWMS).  
* **Disposal Practices:** Historically dominated by open dumps and agricultural drainage dumping. Current projects are focused on upgrading these to engineered sanitary landfills.  
* **Priority Actions:**  
  * Bridge the 35% funding gap through new electricity-bill-linked waste fees.  
  * Modernize 65+ government composting facilities to handle the 50-60% organic waste fraction.  
  * Scale up formal recycling from the current 2-4% to national targets.

### **NIGERIA**

* **Overview:** Nigeria generates \~32 million tonnes of waste per year, with Lagos alone contributing 13,000 tonnes daily. The 2025 focus is on the "Blue Economy" and aggressive plastic bans.  
* **Collection Systems:** National coverage is low (20-30%), though Lagos has a more structured private-sector participation (PSP) model. Significant "leakage" occurs in suburbs, leading to drainage blockages.  
* **Recycling and Innovation:** Recycling rates are low (\~13% in Lagos), but 2025 marks a turning point with a total ban on single-use plastic bags, straws, and styrofoam. New USD 116M Waste-to-Energy facilities (e.g., Phoenix Edison) are entering the market.  
* **Disposal Practices:** Relies on massive open dumpsites (like Olusosun) that face challenges with methane fires and air pollution.  
* **Priority Actions:**  
  * Enforce the January 2025 ban on single-use plastics and styrofoam.  
  * Subsidize the high cost of waste bins to encourage "Source Segregation" among residents.  
  * Expand Material Recovery Facilities (MRFs) to capture the 60% uncollected waste stream.

### **KENYA**

* **Overview:** Operates under a devolved system where 47 counties manage waste. The 2025 landscape is dominated by the Sustainable Waste Management Act of 2022 and efforts to combat the "mitumba" (textile waste) crisis.  
* **Collection Systems:** Coverage is mixed; new 2024 regulations require counties (like Murang'a and Taita Taveta) to provide designated "refuse chambers" and segregated collection services.  
* **Recycling and Innovation:** A leader in plastic bans. Current innovation focuses on the "Five R’s" (Refuse, Reduce, Reuse, Repurpose, Recycle) and upcycling second-hand textiles into new circular fashion products.  
* **Disposal Practices:** Moving away from open dumps (like the hazardous Dandora site) toward engineered landfills and county-level MRFs to support a "Blue Economy."  
* **Priority Actions:**  
  * Operationalize the 2024 Sustainable Waste Management Rules across all counties.  
  * Increase collection coverage from 30% to 50% by building capacity for youth groups and CBOs.  
  * Establish "Textile Take-back" hubs to manage the 22.5 million kg of annual clothing imports.

## **4\. WASTE STREAMS**

### **Meaning of Waste Streams**

A waste stream refers to the flow of specific types of waste from the point of generation through collection, treatment, recycling, and final disposal. Understanding waste streams is essential for designing effective waste management systems because each stream has unique characteristics, risks, and management requirements.

In Africa—particularly in South Africa, Egypt, Nigeria, and Kenya—waste streams are heavily influenced by population growth, urbanization, consumption patterns, and economic development.

### **4.1 Municipal Solid Waste (MSW)**

* **Description:** Consists of waste generated from households, offices, schools, markets, and commercial establishments. It typically includes food waste, paper, plastics, metals, glass, and textiles.  
* **Situation in Africa:**  
  * Africa generated about 125 million tonnes of MSW in 2012, projected to exceed 240 million tonnes by 2025\.  
  * Average MSW generation in developing countries is about 0.6 kg/person/day, compared to 2.1 kg/person/day in high-income countries.  
  * Collection rates in Sub-Saharan Africa remain low (≈ 44%), resulting in large volumes of uncollected waste.  
* **Key Challenges:** Mixed waste collection reduces recycling efficiency; open dumping and burning remain common; poor infrastructure and weak enforcement.  
* **Additional Insight:** Improving MSW management requires source segregation, material recovery facilities (MRFs), and community-based collection systems, especially in informal settlements.

### **4.2 Organic (Biodegradable) Waste**

* **Description:** Includes food scraps, agricultural residues, yard waste, and biodegradable materials.  
* **Situation in Africa:**  
  * Organic waste accounts for approximately **58% of Africa’s total waste stream**.  
  * Egypt’s MSW contains about 56% organic waste, making treatment difficult.  
  * Poor management leads to methane emissions, odors, and disease vectors.  
* **Key Challenges:** High moisture content makes incineration inefficient; lack of composting and anaerobic digestion facilities; organic waste is often mixed with plastics and metals.  
* **Additional Insight:** Represents the highest-value opportunity. Composting and anaerobic digestion (biogas production) can reduce landfill volumes, improve soil fertility, generate renewable energy, and cut greenhouse gas emissions.

### **4.3 Plastic Waste**

* **Description:** Originates mainly from packaging, bottles, shopping bags, and single-use plastics.  
* **Situation in Africa:**  
  * Global plastic waste reached \~220 million tonnes in 2024\.  
  * Recycling rates in Nigeria are often below 10%.  
  * Plastic pollution is severe in urban drainage systems and coastal areas.  
  * Kenya faces rising plastic waste despite bans, while Egypt and Nigeria struggle with enforcement.  
* **Key Challenges:** Non-biodegradability; low market value of some plastics; mixed and contaminated plastics.  
* **Additional Insight:** Solutions include Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), plastic buy-back programs, chemical recycling (plastic-to-fuel), and design for recyclability.

### **4.4 Electronic Waste (E-Waste)**

* **Description:** Includes discarded electronic devices such as phones, computers, TVs, and batteries, often containing hazardous substances.  
* **Situation in Africa:**  
  * Global e-waste reached 62 million tonnes in 2022, growing five times faster than recycling.  
  * Recycling rates in Africa remain extremely low.  
  * Informal dismantling exposes workers to lead, mercury, and toxic fumes.  
* **Key Challenges:** Lack of formal recycling facilities; informal processing using unsafe methods; weak enforcement of import controls.  
* **Additional Insight:** Proper management requires formal take-back schemes, certified recycling plants, public awareness on safe disposal, and enforcement of Basel Convention regulations.

### **4.5 Hazardous and Industrial Waste**

* **Description:** Includes industrial chemicals, medical waste, mining residues, and toxic by-products.  
* **Situation in Africa:** Often mixed with municipal waste; poor treatment facilities; high environmental and health risks.  
* **Key Challenges:** Inadequate monitoring and disposal systems; illegal dumping; limited treatment infrastructure.  
* **Additional Insight:** Best practices include dedicated hazardous waste landfills, incineration with emission controls, and strict regulatory oversight.

### **4.6 Textile Waste (Emerging Stream)**

* **Description:** Includes discarded clothing and fabric materials, increasingly linked to fast fashion and second-hand clothing imports (mitumba).  
* **Situation in Africa:** Kenya faces rising textile waste volumes. Large quantities end up in dumpsites like Dandora. Limited reuse and recycling pathways.  
* **Additional Insight:** Solutions include textile take-back programs, repair and upcycling hubs, and circular fashion initiatives.

### **4.7 Cross-Cutting Waste Stream Issues**

* **Informal sector dominance:** Plays a key role in recycling but operates under unsafe conditions.  
* **Mixed waste collection:** Limits recycling potential.  
* **Data gap:** Inconsistent waste data affects planning.  
* **Environmental leakage:** Uncollected waste enters waterways and ecosystems.

## **5\. TECHNOLOGIES AND INNOVATIONS**

### **🇿🇦 SOUTH AFRICA**

1. **Smart Waste Monitoring & IoT Technology:** IoT Sensors and Smart Bins are being adopted to monitor waste levels in real-time, optimizing collection dispatch and cutting fuel use.  
2. **Recycling Incentives & Startups:**  
   * **Regenize:** Incentivized recycling rewarding households and pickers via mobile tech.  
   * **Boomba.mobi:** Platform linking waste collection to food/essentials exchange; includes small-scale organic processing like worm farms.  
3. **Waste-to-Energy & Circular Solutions:** **Kibo Gauteng Thermal Power Station** plans to convert plastic waste into energy (syngas and electricity) using pyrolysis.  
4. **Community & Policy Innovations:** Government policies support separation-at-source initiatives to divert waste from landfills.

### **🇰🇪 KENYA**

1. **Pneumatic Waste Collection – Konza Technopolis:** Africa’s first underground network replacing trucks with vacuum tubes to move waste to central sorting.  
2. **Waste-to-Energy Projects:** **Kakamega Waste-to-Energy Plant** (under construction) will incinerate solid waste to generate \~10 MW of electricity.  
3. **Innovative Recycling & Circular Ventures:**  
   * **HyaPak:** Converts invasive water hyacinth into biodegradable packaging.  
   * **EcoPost:** Processes plastics into durable building products (eco-fencing).  
   * **Mr. Green Africa:** Tech-enabled plastics recycling formalizing recovery supply chains.

### **🇪🇬 EGYPT**

1. **Zabbaleen Informal Recycling System:** Local communities in Cairo recycle up to 80% of collected waste through highly efficient manual and semi-mechanized sorting.  
2. **Digital and Circular Partnerships:** Digital credit schemes reward informal collectors for plastic waste, formalizing the value chain.  
3. **Government Reforms:** Expanding capacity with new plants producing organic fertilizer, hydrogen, and advanced materials like graphene.  
4. **Informal Sector Integration:** Programs providing training and logistics support to traditional Zabbaleen workers.

### **🇳🇬 NIGERIA**

1. **Social Enterprise Models:** **Wecyclers** uses a points-based SMS system and low-cost cargo bikes to link households to recycling markets.  
2. **Recycling Startups:**  
   * **Vicfold Recyclers:** Converts plastic waste into resins for manufacturing.  
   * **Salubata:** Transforms plastic waste into modular shoes.  
3. **Community Platforms:** Using mobile tech and SMS to make recycling accessible for low-income households.

## **6\. POLICIES AND REGULATIONS**

### **🇿🇦 SOUTH AFRICA**

* **National Environmental Management: Waste Act (NEMWA), 2008:** Primary national law governing classification, handling, and disposal.  
* **National Waste Management Strategy (NWMS) 2020:** Implements integrated plans focused on reducing generation and promoting recycling.  
* **Waste Classification & Management Regulations:** Categorizes types of waste and handling definitions.  
* **National Environmental Management Act (NEMA), 1998:** Overarching environmental framework.

### **🇰🇪 KENYA**

* **Environmental Management and Co-ordination Act (EMCA):** Governs waste and pollution control.  
* **EPR Regulations 2020:** Introduces Extended Producer Responsibility for products and packaging.  
* **Sustainable Waste Management Regulations (2025):** Emphasizes compliance for EPR schemes and licensing for facilities.  
* **Plastic & Single-Use Plastic Bans:** Strict bans with criminal penalties for non-compliance.

### **🇳🇬 NIGERIA**

* **NESREA Act, 2007:** Establishes the main enforcement agency for environmental standards.  
* **National Environmental (Sanitation and Wastes Control) Regulations (2009):** Primary legal framework for sanitation.  
* **Sector-Specific Regulations:** Covering healthcare, electronic, and hazardous waste (Battery Control Regulations).  
* **Waste-Related Bans:** State-level bans (e.g., Lagos) on single-use plastics and styrofoam.

### **🇪🇬 EGYPT**

* **Waste Management Law No. 202 of 2020:** Comprehensive law establishing a national system for handling and treatment.  
* **Executive Regulations (2022):** Details on licensing and roles for public/private sectors.  
* **Incentive Mechanisms:** Tax incentives for waste management companies.  
* **Prime Ministerial Decree No. 662 of 2025:** Implementing EPR for plastics and bag fees.
